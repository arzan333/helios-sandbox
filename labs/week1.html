Helios Week 1 lab book Training environment

Designing AI-Native Workflows

Self-paced. Work through it in order. Every command has a copy button.

Time 45 minutes You produce week1/blueprint.md Committed locally, never pushed Needs Claude Code, Python, this repository

What you will do

In this lab you will, in order:

  1. Analyse real ticket data with Claude Code to find the biggest bottlenecks in a business process.
  2. Categorise every step as AI-ready, AI-assisted or human-only.
  3. Write down the redesigned process — what changed and who does each step now.
  4. Insert mandatory human approval gates, each with an owner role and pass/fail rules, so AI output is validated before it moves on.
  5. Set target metrics for cycle time and rework, with real numbers.
  6. Commit your blueprint to Git, locally.

The output is one file, week1/blueprint.md.

What a blueprint actually is. Your team is about to start using Claude Code on real delivery work. Today every person decides for themselves how much to hand over, and nobody writes it down. A blueprint is the team's answer for one process: AI drafts these steps, a person still decides those, and here is who signs off before work continues. One page. It settles three arguments that otherwise happen every week — can I let Claude write this, who is accountable when its output is wrong, and did any of this actually help.
One process, not the whole project. Helios has four slow processes. You blueprint one today — request to release — and take a second on your own afterwards. In a real organisation you would not blueprint everything: only the paths most of your work travels through, that run often enough to matter, and that end in something a human is accountable for.
Nothing runs at the end of today. A blueprint is a decision written down, not automation. That comes next: Week 3 you cost it against real volumes and ship a real change through it, Week 4 you turn your gate criteria into a Skill that runs the review on demand. A vague blueprint becomes a vague cost model and a useless Skill, so it is worth doing properly rather than quickly.
See a finished one first — two minutes.
Open docs/examples/blueprint-defect-triage.md. It is a completed blueprint for a different Helios process. Yours will look like that. You cannot copy it, but you can see the depth expected.

The process you are analysing

Helios Group is a fictional consumer products company. Its delivery team maintains four services: a React storefront, a Python order API, Python reporting jobs and a Java invoicing service. You have just joined. Nobody on your team wrote this code and there is no architect to ask — the repository is your only source of truth.

The process you are redesigning is request to release: the sixteen steps between someone asking for a change and it being live. Every change to this codebase travels that path, including the one you will make in Week 3.

The data you will analyse

data/helios-tickets.csv holds 210 tickets from the last six months, 91 of them request-to-release. Each row records the moment a ticket entered every stage, plus whether it went backwards and why. One real row:

HEL-005
title            Capture marketing consent at account creation
created          2026-02-16 15:00
analysis_done    2026-02-18 14:52
design_start     2026-02-27 12:53   <- nine days later
build_start      2026-03-03 09:59
build_done       2026-03-05 17:10   <- two days of building
test_start       2026-03-11 09:24   <- six days later
closed           2026-03-13 11:09
rework_count     1
rework_reason    acceptance criteria missing

Twenty-five days from raised to closed. Two of them spent building. The rest is mostly waiting, and it went backwards once because nobody wrote down what "done" meant. Subtracting one timestamp from the next, across all 91 tickets, is how you find the bottlenecks in step 1.

1Find where the process loses its time 0–9 min

The process has sixteen steps. Before deciding anything about them, find out which ones actually consume the time. Run this in a second terminal, not the one running Claude Code:

powershell 
cd $env:USERPROFILE\Helios\helios-sandbox
python apps/insight/report.py stages --workflow request_to_release
Expected. Eleven stage transitions with a median and p90, the three longest by median, your end-to-end median, the rework rate and the top rework reasons. Leave this on screen — you will check Claude's answers against it twice.

Now have Claude Code interpret it rather than just repeat it:

prompt 
Read data/helios-tickets.csv and docs/process/request-to-release.md.
For the request_to_release workflow only, give me the three longest stage transitions by
median duration. For each one, say whether it is a queue (the ticket is waiting
for a person or an environment) or effort (someone is actively working), and
cite the step number from the narrative that explains it.
Answer as a table, nothing else.
Queue or effort. A queue is a ticket sitting still. Effort is someone working. It matters because a queue is fixed by changing the process, and effort is not — pointing an agent at genuine effort just produces faster wrong answers. Two of the three here are queues and one is effort. If Claude calls all three waits, it has guessed instead of reading. Make it justify each against the narrative.

Write into section 1 of your blueprint: the three worst transitions, with the median and p90 the report gave you, and whether each is a queue or effort. You now know where the process is slow and why. Step 2 is where you decide what to do about it.

2Decide what AI can touch 9–19 min
The three categories. AI-ready — the job is bounded (you can state exactly what done looks like), checkable (you could tell if it were wrong), and reversible (a mistake breaks nothing permanently). All three, or it is not AI-ready. AI-assisted — AI drafts, a person finishes and owns it. Human-only — keep AI out of the decision.

Now go through the process step by step and decide, for each one, whether AI can do it, help with it, or must stay out of it.

Typing sixteen rows by hand is not the skill, so have Claude build the empty grid first:

prompt 
Read docs/process/request-to-release.md and open week1/blueprint.md.
In section 2 of week1/blueprint.md, replace the empty table with one row for
each of the sixteen steps. Columns: #, Step, Bounded?, Checkable?,
Reversible?, Category, Reason.
Fill in only the # and Step columns from the narrative. Leave the other five
columns empty for me to complete. Do not categorise anything yourself.
Expected. Sixteen rows in week1/blueprint.md, numbered and named, with five blank columns. If Claude filled in the categories, tell it to clear them — that judgement is the exercise.

Now do the thinking. Work down the table in VS Code. For each step, answer the three questions with yes or no, then let those answers decide the category: all three yes is AI-ready, mixed is AI-assisted, and a judgement call or anything irreversible is human-only. Write a one-line reason for each. "It would be faster" is not a reason — say what makes the step bounded and checkable, or what makes it a judgement call.

Then have Claude challenge you. Save the file first so it can read your work:

prompt 
Read section 2 of week1/blueprint.md.
If the Category column is still empty, stop and tell me - I have not saved my
work yet.
Otherwise, for each filled row, argue the opposite case in one sentence.
Where my reason is really just "it would be faster" restated, say so plainly.
Where I marked a step AI-ready but being wrong is not actually reversible,
say so.
Do not edit the file and do not rewrite my table. Reply as a list only.
Expected. Sixteen counter-arguments in the chat, your file untouched. Change your own rows only where you actually agree.
Watch for. Steps that are queues, not tasks. Step 8 is the ticket waiting for the architect. No category fixes that — it is a process problem, and you deal with it in step 3.

Now fill in the Change column yourself. Most rows are Unchanged. You are looking for the few that are not — where handing a step to AI makes a neighbouring step pointless. Ask Claude to find the candidates:

prompt 
Read section 2 of week1/blueprint.md and docs/process/request-to-release.md.
List only the steps where my AI-ready or AI-assisted verdict makes a neighbouring
step redundant or mergeable, and say which neighbour and why, in one line each.
If there are none, say so.

Expected. A short list, usually two or three candidates. Decide which you accept, then write Removed or Merged into step N in the Change column for those rows and Unchanged for the rest.
Most steps should say Unchanged. If half your list is Removed, you have redesigned a process you do not work in. Leaving a step alone is a decision, and section 3 is where you record that you made it.
Context, not a choice to make. The sixteen steps run through four phases, and each phase has a shape: steps 1–7 produce a requirements document and steps 8–11 produce a design, both Draft-Review-Approve. Steps 12–14 produce code and tests, Generate-Verify-Publish. Steps 15–16 release it, Analyze-Validate-Escalate. Use this to sense-check your table: a step that produces a document needs a reviewer, a step that produces code needs a verifier.
Anti-pattern. AI at an approval step. AI drafts and analyses; a named human approves. If any row says the approval itself is done by AI, change it.
4Add the human sign-offs 26–36 min
What a gate is. A point where work stops until one named person says yes. Not a meeting, not "the team reviews it". One role, one decision, and a written answer to what happens when they say no.

Your new process has AI doing work that a person used to do. Every one of those steps needs someone to check the output before it moves on.

Every step you marked AI-ready or AI-assisted gets a gate after it. At least two in total. Fill in section 4 — all six columns, no blanks:

ColumnWhat it must say
GateA short name for it, so you can refer to it later — "Acceptance criteria check"
Placed after stepThe step number from the narrative
Owner roleOne of: Business Analyst, Developer, Architect, Quality Engineer, Release Manager. A team is not an owner. See docs/helios-landscape.md
Pass criteriaWhat has to be true to move on — testable, not "looks fine"
Failure handlingWhere it goes back to, and what gets recorded
Escalation triggerWhat makes this stop being the owner's decision

What the architect actually left out (open only after you have decided)

No out-of-scope statement. Insight reads order data and is never mentioned. The spreadsheet import has no owner. "About three days" has no basis. The one risk has no response and no owner. No alternative was considered. The review_date is unset. None of that makes it obviously a fail — it makes it unreviewable, which is the actual problem.

For each rework reason above, say which of my gates would catch it and which

Check these against your step 1 output. If they disagree, Claude is wrong — the report reads the whole file, and it is the ground truth.

Put the cycle time and rework rate in section 5 with a target beside each, and say how each will be measured. A target without a number is a wish. Read the rework reasons as a list of information the process failed to collect — that is what your gates are aimed at.

Challenge — swap and critique

10 minutes, in pairs, after the lab

Trade blueprints with the person next to you. You are not marking their work. You are looking for the three things that make a blueprint useless to somebody else:

  1. Could you start work from this? Take any step they marked AI-ready. Do they say what the agent is given and what it produces? If you would have to ask a question first, write down the question.
  2. Would you know if it went wrong? Take one of their gates. Does it name one person and say what happens on failure? A team name or a blank failure column both mean nobody owns it.
  3. Where is that number from? Pick a figure in their section 5 and ask which command produced it. If they cannot show you, it is an estimate wearing a number's clothes.

Write your three answers at the bottom of their blueprint and hand it back. Fix what you agree with before you commit.

Passing bar

Every AI-ready and AI-assisted step has a gate after it. Every gate names one role and says what happens when the check fails. Every metric in section 5 has a number that came from the CSV. If a facilitator asks where a number came from, you can show them the command.
