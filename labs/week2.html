Helios Week 2 lab book Training environment

One Task, Three Prompt Sizes

Self-paced. Follow along on your own machine, or watch and run it afterwards. Every command has a copy button.

Time 45 minutes You produce a ledger of what each run cost, three scored answers, and a published page Committed locally, never pushed Needs Claude Code 2.1.211+, Python, this repository
The job. A ticket asks for a delivery estimate on the cart page. It has no acceptance criteria, so nobody can build it or test it. Writing those criteria is today's job - and you will do it three times, with three instructions that ask for exactly the same thing at three very different lengths.

The question. The long instruction feels safer. Does it buy a better answer, or just a more expensive one? By the end you will have your own answer, as a table.

The shape of the hour

StepWhat you do
1See what a session costs before you type anything
2Read the three instructions side by side, and predict which will win
3Run the long one by hand, and record what it cost
4Run the other two - one command each
5Have a stronger model score all three answers
6Turn the winner into a page, then change it twice
7Commit

Every run pays for three things: the overhead Claude always carries (cached and cheap, but re-carried on every file it opens), the input - your instruction plus everything it reads - and the output, everything it writes back, which costs several times what input does.

Three other ways teams waste tokens, in one paragraph each

Running the same wrong thing again. Three attempts at an instruction that was never going to work cost three times and teach nothing. Change the input before you re-run, not just your luck.

Letting a session grow. Everything said so far is re-sent on every request. Clear it when you move to an unrelated job; compact it when you need the thread but not the detail.

Handing work between sessions. Whoever picks the work up starts from nothing and pays for the context again. It is why a chain of four steps can cost far more than the same job done once - and why next week prices a chain before anyone builds one.

2Read the three instructions 4-12 min

Why. If you do not know what is different about them, the numbers in step 5 are just numbers. These eight minutes are what make the rest of the hour mean something.

All three ask for the same deliverable: acceptance criteria for the ticket, taken from the ticket itself and one section of the product spec, written to a file. What differs is how much they say about it.

The long one - about 2,000 tokens

It opens like this:

from the long instruction
You are a world-class senior business analyst with more than twenty years of
experience writing requirements and acceptance criteria for complex e-commerce,
order management and logistics systems at large enterprises. You have deep
expertise in BDD, Gherkin, Given/When/Then, INVEST, user story mapping, and
modern agile delivery practices. You are meticulous, thorough, careful and
detail-oriented, and you never miss an edge case...

Then it pastes the whole ticket in - and afterwards asks Claude to go and read that same ticket:

from the long instruction
{
  "key": "HEL-142",
  "summary": "Show estimated delivery window in the cart",
  ... the whole ticket, about 30 lines ...
}

To be thorough, please also read docs/design/HEL-142-hld.md (even though I
pasted it above, in case it has changed), data/entity-map.md,
docs/helios-landscape.md and docs/process/request-to-release.md, so that you
have complete and total context... It is better to read too much than too little.

And it asks for six separate things, most of which nobody will read:

from the long instruction
First, please write a short executive summary... Second, please explain your
reasoning step by step... Third, please produce the acceptance criteria
themselves... and for each one please also add a paragraph of prose explaining
the criterion... Fourth, please add a section on risks and assumptions. Fifth,
please add a section listing any open questions. Finally, please add a brief
conclusion...

Please write the whole thing to the file week2/ac-v2000.md... Then, in the chat,
please give me a full summary of everything you wrote.
Four kinds of waste, in one instruction. A role paragraph that instructs nothing. Content pasted and read. Four more files "to be thorough". And an output list that pays for reasoning, prose and a conclusion - then a full recap in the chat of the file it just wrote. Reading is charged once, at input rates. That recap is charged again, at output rates.

The medium one - about 900 tokens

Same job. It names its inputs instead of pasting them, and says what it wants back:

from the medium instruction
Read these inputs. Do not paste them back to me.

1. helios-backlog/HEL-142.json - the ticket. Note that acceptance_criteria is
   empty; that is what we are fixing.
2. docs/specs/ordercore-fsd.txt - the rules for this feature are in section 7...
3. docs/design/HEL-142-hld.md - the draft design. It is not approved. If it
   disagrees with the specification, the specification wins...

When you have written the file, reply in the chat with one line: the number of
criteria and the number of open questions. Nothing else.

It still carries a paragraph of background, explains itself as it goes, and leaves Claude to decide how much of the document to read.

The short one - about 320 tokens. This is all of it

the entire short instruction
Write acceptance criteria for HEL-142 to week2/ac-v320.md.

Inputs:
- helios-backlog/HEL-142.json
- docs/specs/ordercore-fsd.txt, section 7 only. Find the line of the heading
  "7. Cart and delivery window" with grep, then read from that line to the
  heading "8. Order search and reporting". Do not read the rest of the file.

Output file, and nothing else:
- Line 1: "# HEL-142 - acceptance criteria"
- A table with columns: # | Given | When | Then | Source (FSD 7.x)
- 6 to 9 rows. Cover: window shown before confirm; display format, one row each
  for same month, cross month and same day, using the exact strings from 7.3 as
  the Then; cut-off rule (before and after 16:00); region transit (one mainland,
  one non-mainland row from Table 5); window unavailable case, with the exact
  fallback text from 7.4; banned hedging words from 7.3.
- "## Open questions": bullets for what the ticket or section 7 leaves
  undecided. List, do not answer.

Rules: every Then is observable by a tester on the cart screen. Where the
specification gives an exact string, the Then is that string. Do not invent
rules outside the ticket and section 7. Do not decide where stock data lives.
No prose, no summary.

Reply in chat with one line: number of rows and number of open questions.
Notice what the short one is not. It is not vaguer. It is more specific than the long one about the only things that matter - what the answer must contain, and what it must not invent. What it dropped was everything that was not an instruction.

3Run the long one, by hand 12-20 min

Why. One run done the slow way, so that when a command does it for you in the next step, you know exactly what it did.

a. Put the long instruction on the clipboard:

powershell

b. In the Claude Code window, empty the session first:

claude code 
/clear
Expected. The conversation disappears and you are back to an empty prompt. Nothing else happens - /clear only resets the session, and with it the figures you are about to read.

c. Now paste the instruction with Ctrl+V and press Enter.

Expected. The paste appears as one collapsed line - [Pasted text #1 +96 lines] - then Claude works. Count the files it opens: that is the part you are paying for. Say Yes if it asks to write its answer. It finishes with a long chat summary, because the instruction asked for one. Wait for the input prompt to return.
Do not chat with it. Any extra exchange lands in this run's numbers. If the file got written, the run is done.

d. Ask what it cost. /usage, then select the Usage by model line with the mouse and copy it (Ctrl+Shift+C), then Esc:

How to read it. The long instruction usually scores well on coverage and badly on inventing things - it read eight files and had opinions about all of them. The short one usually holds, occasionally dropping one point of coverage. So: a big saving for one lost point is a win. A big saving for an answer that quietly made up a rule is a loss, and the instruction goes back for another pass. Either is a fine outcome. Not knowing which you got is the only bad one - and with one run each, what you can honestly claim is the direction, not the decimal.
6Turn the winner into a page, then change it twice 34-42 min

Why. Deliverables get revised. Revising by asking for the whole thing again pays the full price every time; changing what is already there costs a fraction. This step measures that gap.

What a published page is. A file in your repository that Claude puts on a private web page and updates in place - same link, new version, each time. If your session cannot publish, Claude just writes the file: open it in a browser and refresh after each change. The lesson is the same either way.

Challenge - how noisy is one run?

5 minutes, in pairs, afterwards

Put your table next to your neighbour's. Same instructions, same guide, different numbers. Pick one row and work out why: a different set of files opened, a longer reply, a different verdict? Then run the short instruction a second time as --version v320b. The gap between your own two runs is your noise floor, and any difference smaller than it is not a finding.

Passing bar

Five rows in your ledger, every figure measured rather than guessed. Three answers scored by a stronger model than wrote them, with one row checked by you. You can say in one sentence what the short instruction lost, or did not. A page built once and changed twice, with the cost of the changes recorded. All committed locally.
