Cost the Tool Chain

On your own. 45 minutes. Nothing here depends on the lab, and step 0 fetches everything you need. Every command has a copy button.

Time 45 minutes You produce a filled tool chain canvas, a cost model with real figures in it, and one chain priced twice Committed locally, never pushed Needs Claude Code 2.1.211 or later, Python 3.11, VS Code, this repository
What you are pricing. Helios automates its monthly release notes with four AI steps in a row. It works. It has never been costed, and nobody has ever asked whether all four steps need a model at all. That chain is written down, with measured token counts, in docs/process/over-engineered-chain.md.

The claim on trial. That a chain built because the work felt like four steps costs several times what the same work costs done once. You are going to put a number on it, twice, and end with a sentence you could defend to a finance reviewer.
Did you miss the lab? Nothing below needs it. The lab changed some code and gated it; this hands-on prices a workflow. They share only the folder they write into.

2A tier for each step, and a reason 7-14 min

Why. Every step here runs on the most expensive tier because nobody chose. A tier without a rationale is not a decision, it is a default with a price on it.

The three tiers, from data/model-pricing.csv, captured 1 September 2026:

TierModelInput, per millionOutput, per millionWhat it is for
fastHaiku 4.5$1.00$5.00Classification, extraction, routing. High volume, low judgement

deepOpus 5$15.00$75.00Design reasoning, adversarial review, marking other models' work

A rationale has to answer three things at once, or it is a preference: quality - what breaks if the answer is a bit worse; latency - who is waiting; volume - how often it runs. Release notes go out once a month, so nobody is waiting and volume is one. That leaves quality doing all the work, which is exactly the case where deep is easiest to justify and hardest to justify honestly.

3Does this step need AI at all? 14-21 min

Why. The cheapest model is no model. A step you delete does not need a tier, a fallback, a retry allowance or a prompt, and it never gets the answer wrong.

The three questions. Ask them of every step. Three yeses and it is not an AI step:
  • Is the output decided by a rule you could write down? If you can say the rule in a sentence, code can follow it.
  • Is the input already structured? A CSV with a date column does not need reading comprehension.
  • Would two people agree on the right answer without discussing it? If there is nothing to argue about, there is no judgement to buy.
A deterministic replacement has to be named - a script, a query, a regular expression, a linter. "Automate it" is not an alternative.

Why. Everything so far is an argument. This step turns it into a number that somebody outside the team can check.

6Fallbacks, then the anti-pattern audit 36-42 min

Why. A design that only works when everything is available is a demonstration. Naming what happens when it is not is what makes it a design.

A fallback names a thing, the way a deterministic alternative does. "Retry" is not a fallback - it is the same call with the same input, which is anti-pattern three. A fallback is another tier, a deterministic route, a queue until tomorrow, or a person.

Three are in the document in black and white. Context dumping: step 1 is handed the whole dataset and step 2 is handed step 1's output in full. Duplicate derivation: step 3 re-reads the dataset step 1 already read. Premature chaining: four sessions, four context bills, and the document says the split was never revisited.

Retry without change and redundant verification have no evidence either way - the document never says what happens when a step fails, or whether anything checks anything. "No evidence" is the honest answer to both. Over-prompting is the only arguable fourth: step 2 writes a paragraph per ticket that step 4 then rewrites, which hints at an instruction carrying more than it needs. To call it a yes you would have to read the step 2 prompt itself, and the document does not include it. Say that, and it is a good answer at 3 or at 4. A count of six means the audit was written rather than run.

Take it back to your desk

15 minutes, optional

Everything above priced a chain somebody else built inside a company that does not exist. The version that pays is the one you run on a workflow of your own. It is the same four moves and it fits in a quarter of an hour:

MinutesDo this
0-4Draw your workflow as a list of steps and mark the ones where a model is called. Most people find one they had forgotten
4-8Run the three questions on each. Cross out the ones that are a rule over structured data, and name what replaces them
8-12Put what is left into a copy of the cost model with your own token counts and your own monthly volume
12-15Write the one sentence. If you cannot name the number, you are not finished, you are guessing

The workbook needs only Python and openpyxl, and the canvas is a Markdown file, so both copy into any repository. The thing worth carrying is not either file - it is the habit of asking whether a step needs a model before asking which one.

Passing bar

A task type named at all four AI steps, each with a clause of reasoning. A tier at each, each with a rationale that mentions quality, latency and volume, and at least one tier you chose rather than accepted. At least one step failing the "does this need AI" test and replaced by something named. The chain priced as designed and priced again redesigned, both figures traceable to a file. A fallback for every remaining step that is not the word "retry". All six anti-pattern rows answered with the step number you checked. One sentence with two numbers in it. All committed locally.
